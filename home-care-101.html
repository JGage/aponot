Home Care 101: What Families Actually Need When a Parent Declines

May 2026 · 5 min read

When a parent starts to need help, most families don't know where to begin. The vocabulary is unfamiliar, the options blur together, and the stakes feel high. This is a plain-language guide to what non-medical home care actually is, what it covers, and when it's the right call.

What "non-medical home care" means

Non-medical home care — sometimes called personal care — is help with the everyday activities that keep someone safe and comfortable at home. It is not nursing or medical treatment. Instead, it covers the practical, physical support an older adult may no longer manage alone. It's delivered by trained caregivers, typically Certified Nursing Assistants (CNAs) or Home Health Aides (HHAs), employed by a licensed agency.

What it typically covers

Common services include help with bathing and hygiene, mobility and transfers (moving safely from bed to chair, for example), medication reminders, meal preparation, light housekeeping, transportation to appointments, and companionship. A good care plan mixes these to match the person — a few hours of help with the morning routine, or fuller daily support after a hospital stay.

One important distinction: caregivers provide medication reminders, not medical administration. Anything clinical — wound care, injections, skilled nursing — falls under home health, a separate, physician-ordered service. Most families needing everyday support are looking for personal care, not skilled nursing.

When to bring it in

Families often wait too long, treating outside help as a last resort rather than a tool. A few common signals that it's time: a recent fall or a close call; noticeable trouble with bathing or dressing; missed or doubled medications; weight loss or an empty fridge; or a primary family caregiver who is visibly exhausted. Bringing in help earlier — even a few hours a week — tends to prevent the crises that force harder, costlier decisions later.

How much it costs

Non-medical home care is usually billed hourly, and rates vary by market and by the quality of the agency. It's paid privately in most cases — traditional health insurance and Medicare generally don't cover ongoing personal care. That's a big part of why arranging it well, at a fair rate, matters so much, and why a benefit that lowers the rate and removes the legwork can make a real difference for a working family.

The reassuring part: with the right agency and a clear plan, home care is one of the most effective ways to keep an aging parent safe, comfortable, and in the home they know.
