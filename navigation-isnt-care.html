Care Navigation Isn't Care: The Gap in Today's Eldercare Benefits

June 2026 · 5 min read

If you look across the eldercare benefits on the market today, most fall into one of three buckets: care navigation, companion visits, and referral lists. Each is useful in its own way. None of them, on their own, delivers the thing a family actually needs when a parent's health turns — hands-on care, in the home, arranged quickly.

Navigation: help thinking, not help doing

Care navigation puts a knowledgeable person on the phone to help an employee understand their options — what kind of care exists, what it costs, what questions to ask. That guidance has real value early on. But at the end of the call, the employee still has to go find an agency, screen it, negotiate a rate, and coordinate a schedule. The hardest, most stressful work is exactly the part navigation hands back to them.

Companion visits: presence, not care

Companion platforms send a friendly person to spend time with an older adult — conversation, a walk, a game of cards. For isolation and loneliness, that's genuinely valuable. But companions are not trained or, in many cases, legally permitted to provide hands-on personal care: bathing, mobility assistance, medication reminders, the tasks that prevent a fall or a hospital visit. When a family hits a real crisis, a companion visit isn't the answer.

Referral lists: a directory, not a solution

A vetted list of local agencies feels like progress — at least the options are pre-screened. But it still leaves the employee doing the calling, comparing, and waiting for callbacks, often during the exact week they have the least time and the most stress. A list is a starting point dressed up as a solution.

The gap that's left

Add these together and a pattern emerges: today's eldercare benefits are very good at helping employees think about care and surprisingly poor at helping them receive it. That gap is fine when there's time to plan. It's a real problem in the moment that matters most — when a parent comes home from the hospital, or a spouse can no longer manage alone, and care is needed this week, not eventually.

Closing that gap means doing the parts everyone else leaves out: matching the family with a licensed agency, arranging the actual caregiver, handling the schedule and the payment, and following up. It's harder to build than a directory or a hotline — which is exactly why so few benefits do it, and why it matters so much when one does.
