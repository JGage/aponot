The Sandwich Generation Is Quietly Reshaping Your Workforce

July 2026 · 5 min read

There is a group of employees inside almost every mid-sized company who are doing two full-time jobs at once. They lead teams, close deals, and hit deadlines — and then they leave the office to manage medications, coordinate doctor's appointments, and worry about whether a parent fell while they were in a meeting. They are the sandwich generation, and they are more common than most employers realize.

According to the Pew Research Center, roughly 47% of adults in their 40s and 50s have a parent aged 65 or older and are either raising a child or supporting a grown one. A meaningful share are actively providing care to that parent. For an employer, that means a large slice of your most experienced, hardest-to-replace talent is quietly carrying a second shift you never see on a timesheet.

Why it stays invisible

Caregiving rarely announces itself. Employees don't file a form when a parent's health turns; they absorb it. They take calls between meetings, step out early, and answer emails at midnight to make up the gap. The work still gets done — until the day it doesn't, and a top performer asks for a leave of absence or hands in a resignation that seems to come out of nowhere.

By the time caregiving becomes visible to an employer, it's usually already a crisis. The distraction, the missed days, and the slow erosion of focus happened long before, spread across months in ways no single manager could connect.

Why it's accelerating

This isn't a temporary blip. The population aged 65 and older is growing faster than any other age group, and the adults most likely to care for them are squarely in their prime working years. As the demographics shift, the share of your workforce touched by eldercare will rise — not fall. Employers who plan for it now will be far better positioned than those who treat each case as a one-off.

What employers can do

The instinct is often to offer flexibility — remote days, a leave policy, an employee assistance line. Those help at the margins, but they don't solve the underlying problem: the employee still has to find, vet, arrange, and manage actual care for their parent, usually while exhausted and under pressure. The most effective response is to remove that burden directly, by giving employees access to real, arranged home care rather than another resource to research on their own.

The sandwich generation isn't a fringe case. It's a growing, largely invisible cost sitting inside your most valuable employees — and it's one of the few workforce problems where a modest, well-designed benefit can make a genuinely outsized difference.
