Why Eldercare Is Becoming the Next Must-Have Employee Benefit

June 2026 · 4 min read

A decade ago, childcare support was a nice-to-have — a differentiator a few forward-thinking employers offered to stand out. Today, it's close to table stakes for any company competing for working parents. Eldercare is now walking the same path, and the demographics suggest it will get there faster.

The reason is simple math. The workforce is aging, and so are its parents. The employees most likely to be caring for an older adult are in their 40s and 50s — the same people companies most want to keep. As that group grows, the pressure to support them shifts from "generous" to "expected."

From navigation to delivery

The first wave of eldercare benefits focused on information: hotlines, care coordinators, and directories that help employees understand their options. That was a reasonable starting point, but it left the hardest part — actually arranging and receiving care — on the employee's plate. The next wave, the one gaining traction now, is about delivery: getting real care into the home, not just handing over a list of numbers to call.

Employers are noticing the difference. A benefit that an employee uses once, in a moment of genuine need, and remembers for years is worth far more than one that quietly goes unused. Eldercare, done right, is the former.

Why brokers are paying attention

For benefits consultants and brokers, eldercare is a rare combination: high emotional salience for employees, low cost to the employer, and very little competition in the "real care" category. It's an easy addition to a benefits package that helps win and retain clients — a differentiator that doesn't require a large budget line or a heavy administrative lift.

The window is open now

Categories like this reward early movers. The employers who added childcare support before it was expected earned goodwill and a recruiting edge; the ones who waited simply caught up. Eldercare is at that same inflection point today. For a modest per-employee cost — comparable to the least expensive benefits on the menu — an employer can offer something employees will genuinely feel.

The question for most organizations isn't whether eldercare becomes a standard benefit. It's whether they lead on it or follow.
